Data-centre operating procedures

An approved procedure is only worth what you can show: that it was followed, step by step.

OPSIGNE walks technicians through your approved procedures (MOP, SOP, EOP) one step at a time, records each critical step in a log where any change can be detected, and answers only from your approved documents.

The problem

The procedure exists. What is missing is the record of what actually happened.

The procedure is written, the work varies

The MOP sits in a binder or a PDF. Under the pressure of a maintenance window, steps get skipped, reordered or done from memory. You find out after the incident, rarely before.

Know-how leaves with the seniors

The real order of actions, the reading that should worry you, the quirk of one particular rack: all of it lives in a few heads. When they change role or leave, only the document remains.

The audit is prepared in a scramble

Rebuilding who did what, when, against which version of the document, from emails, photos and memory takes days, and the result is still open to argument.

Deliberately no figures: we show no statistic unless we can name its source on the page.

How it works

Four ideas, demonstrated on a fictional procedure.

Each block below exists today in a proof demo, on an invented procedure (replacing a UPS power module). It is not a product yet. You can try these ideas in the demo further down.

01

Guided execution, with hold points

The technician sees one step at a time. At hold points they must enter the measured value: out of tolerance, the contingency step defined by the procedure is shown. A second person can be required to verify. Every deviation is recorded with its reason.

Fictional example: reading 70% against a 65% maximum - stop, go to the contingency step.

02

A log where any change can be detected

Each critical step becomes an event linked to the previous one by a SHA-256 hash. Editing a past event breaks the chain, and verification shows it. It is not an unbreakable vault: it is a log that makes tampering visible.

Fictional example: a reading edited afterwards -> verification: chain broken at event 7.

03

Answers only from your approved documents

A question asked mid-task is answered with the text of the approved step, with its identifier, version and step number. If nothing in the approved documents answers it, OPSIGNE refuses rather than guessing.

Fictional example: "What is the tyre pressure of the trolley?" -> "Not covered by your approved procedures, escalate to shift lead."

04

An exportable audit report

For each run: who, when, which procedure version, which readings, which deviations, plus the head hash of the chain, to be kept elsewhere if you wish. JSON and HTML export. The report helps produce evidence; it replaces neither your auditor nor your judgement.

Fictional example: RUN-0001, 13 steps, 1 deviation, head hash shown.

    Safety boundary

    OPSIGNE never controls equipment and never invents steps. The approved procedure is always the authority.

    • No connection to equipmentOPSIGNE sends no command and reads no controller. Values are entered by the person on site.
    • No invented stepsAnswers quote the approved text. No free generation, no rewording that could change the meaning.
    • When in doubt, the procedure winsIf the tool and the approved document seem to disagree, follow the approved document and call the on-call lead.
    • Not a safety systemOPSIGNE replaces neither the site’s safety rules, nor interlocks, nor the judgement of authorised people.

    Who it is for

    For the teams that run the halls.

    Mid-size colocation and sovereign-cloud operators in Europe.

    • Operations managers

      You want the approved procedure to be the one that is executed, on every shift, including when the senior person is away.

    • Shift and team leads

      You brief, supervise and sign off. You want a clear sequence for the technician and a record you do not have to retype.

    • Compliance and internal audit managers

      You need to show how a job went. You want dated, verifiable pieces of evidence, not recollections.
